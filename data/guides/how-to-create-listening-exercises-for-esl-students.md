# How to Create Listening Exercises for ESL Students

A listening exercise works when the student can answer the task **without** having to read the transcript first. The text is the raw material, not the test. This guide walks you through the workflow that experienced ESL teachers use to design listening exercises that actually work in class, from choosing the source text to writing questions and using audio tools.

## 1. Pick the source text first, the level second

Most teachers start by choosing a level and then hunting for material. Try the opposite: choose a short, natural-sounding piece of English (a dialogue, a podcast excerpt, a story, a news item) and then check whether it fits the level you teach.

A piece "fits" the level when:

- the vocabulary is mostly familiar, with a small number of new items
- the sentences are short enough to be processed in real time
- the situation is relevant to the learners' lives or interests

If you need to lower the level, simplify. If you need to raise it, slow it down. Resist the temptation to rewrite the text in a textbook style — natural English is what learners will hear outside the classroom.

## 2. Decide on one clear listening goal

For each exercise, write down **one** thing the student should be able to do after listening. Examples:

- Find specific information ("Where is the train station?")
- Understand the main idea ("Why is the customer calling?")
- Infer meaning from context ("How does Ben feel about moving?")
- Notice a specific form ("Listen for the present continuous.")

If your exercise has three listening goals, split it into three exercises. Students perform better on focused tasks, and you will get clearer feedback on whether the activity worked.

## 3. Pre-teach only what blocks comprehension

You should pre-teach vocabulary **only when** the meaning is essential to the task and **cannot** be guessed from context. Two or three words per exercise is usually enough.

Do **not** pre-teach words that the listener can guess from:

- pictures or gestures
- the rest of the sentence ("She opened the *umbrella* because it was raining.")
- cognates with the learners' first language

Over-pre-teaching turns the listening task into a reading task. The point of listening is that the student hears the language used naturally and at speed.

## 4. Structure the listening in stages

Most classroom listening works best in three stages:

1. **Pre-listening (1–2 minutes)** — set the context, ask a quick prediction question, pre-teach blocking vocabulary.
2. **While-listening (2–3 minutes)** — students answer the task. Play the audio **once** for gist and **once** for detail.
3. **Post-listening (3–5 minutes)** — check answers, discuss, then move to a productive task (role-play, written response, debate).

If you only have time for one play, prefer a focused detail task over a vague gist task. Gist questions like "What is the dialogue about?" are too easy to guess and do not produce useful listening practice.

## 5. Write tasks in the order students can answer them

Good listening tasks follow the **order of the audio**. If the answer to question 3 comes after the answer to question 4 in the audio, students will jump back and forth and lose track. Order your questions the way the information actually appears.

Common task types that work well in class:

- **Multiple choice** — easy to mark, focuses attention on specific words.
- **True / False** — good for detail and grammar in context.
- **Gap-fill** — excellent for noticing verb forms or key vocabulary.
- **Short-answer questions** — closer to real-life listening but harder to mark.
- **Ordering events** — strong for narrative or instructional texts.

For A1–A2 learners, prefer multiple choice and true/false. For B1+, add short-answer questions and ordering tasks.

## 6. Use audio you can actually control

A classroom listening exercise is only useful if the teacher can:

- play it more than once
- pause it
- slow it down for difficult sections
- replay specific lines
- use a consistent voice across exercises

If you use pre-recorded audio from a textbook, you lose those controls, and you are stuck with whatever accent and speed the publisher chose.

## 7. Make transcripts visible **after** listening

A transcript is a teaching tool, not a crutch. Show it **after** the listening task is complete, and use it to:

- clarify the parts students asked about
- highlight useful collocations or grammar
- prepare students for a follow-up speaking task

If you show the transcript before listening, you have converted a listening exercise into a reading exercise with sound effects. That can be useful sometimes, but it should be a deliberate choice, not the default.

## 8. Follow up with a productive task

Listening is a receptive skill. To make it stick, pair it with a productive task that uses the same language:

- A role-play that re-uses the dialogue structure
- A writing prompt that asks students to respond to the situation
- A debate based on an opinion expressed in the audio

Without this step, listening tends to evaporate from memory as soon as the bell rings.

## Common pitfalls

- **Too many questions.** Three to five focused tasks per minute of audio is the upper limit for most learners.
- **Questions that test vocabulary they have never seen.** Always check that the words in your questions are known.
- **No clear signal of when to start listening.** Always give a one-sentence context before pressing play.
- **Using authentic audio at full speed with beginners.** Authentic audio is great, but you can slow it down for first exposure.

## A reusable checklist

Before you press play in class, ask yourself:

1. What is the single listening goal?
2. Have I pre-taught only the blocking vocabulary?
3. Are my questions in the order the audio provides the answers?
4. Have I planned what to do with the transcript afterwards?
5. Is there a productive follow-up task?

If you can answer "yes" to all five, the exercise will almost certainly work.
