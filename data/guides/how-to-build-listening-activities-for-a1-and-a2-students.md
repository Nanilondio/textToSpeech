# How to Build Listening Activities for A1 and A2 Students

Listening is the first receptive skill beginners develop, but it is also the easiest one to teach badly. This guide focuses on what actually works for A1 and A2 learners: how to choose texts, what tasks to use, and how to keep beginners engaged without overwhelming them.

## The beginner-listening problem

Beginners face three problems at once:

1. **Vocabulary load.** They know fewer words, so even simple sentences contain unknown items.
2. **Processing speed.** They cannot decode natural-speed English yet.
3. **Working memory.** They forget the beginning of a sentence by the time they reach the end.

A good listening activity for beginners **reduces these three problems in the right order**. Trying to fix all three at once produces an activity that is too easy to teach anything.

## Choose texts that travel well

For A1 learners, a good text:

- Is **very short** — 30 to 60 words.
- Uses **mostly high-frequency vocabulary** (the top 500–1000 words).
- Has **short sentences** with clear word boundaries.
- Sounds natural but not too fast.

For A2 learners, you can extend to:

- 60 to 120 words.
- Vocabulary from the top 1500 words plus a few targeted new items.
- Slightly longer sentences with one or two connectors (and, but, because).

Avoid poetry, song lyrics with irregular rhythm, or anything with heavy cultural references. Beginners benefit from texts that are **everyday, concrete, and predictable in structure**.

## Two-voice dialogues work better than monologues

For beginners, a conversation between two people is almost always easier to follow than a monologue. The reasons:

- **Turn-taking helps working memory.** The listener gets a pause after every speaker turn.
- **Clarification is built in.** Speakers in real conversations rephrase, confirm, and ask each other to repeat.
- **Voice contrast helps.** When Speaker 1 and Speaker 2 have different voices, the listener can follow who is talking without visual cues.

This is one of the most practical reasons to use a text-to-speech tool that lets you assign different voices to different speakers. A single voice reading both sides of a conversation sounds unnatural and makes it harder to follow.

## Tasks that work for beginners

### Pictures first

Show 3–4 pictures. Students listen and point to the matching picture. This is the most accessible task type and works from the very first lesson. Even with no vocabulary yet, students can match pictures to sound.

### True / False

Three to five statements about the dialogue. After listening, students decide whether each is true or false. False statements should be plausible — they should test something the listener might genuinely have missed, not be obviously absurd.

### Multiple choice

The strongest task type for A1–A2. Three options per question. The wrong options should be similar enough to the right one to require real listening, not guessing.

### Ordering

Cut the dialogue into sentences and ask students to put them in the correct order. Works well as a follow-up to a comprehension task.

### Gap-fill (key words)

Provide the dialogue with 4–6 words removed. Students listen and write the missing words. Focus gaps on one feature per task (articles, prepositions, or key verbs).

## The pre-teach, listen, check loop

A reliable sequence for a 10-minute listening activity:

1. **Pre-teach (2 min).** Show 2–3 blocking words with a picture or a quick demonstration. Do not pre-teach words the students can guess from context.
2. **Set the task (1 min).** Tell the students exactly what they need to do. Read the questions aloud.
3. **First listen (2 min).** Students listen for the gist. No writing required.
4. **Second listen (3 min).** Students answer the task while listening. Pause the audio if needed.
5. **Check (2 min).** Compare answers in pairs, then as a class. Highlight the language point you wanted to teach.

The whole sequence fits in one lesson slot and gives beginners enough exposure to make progress.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Too many questions.** Three to four questions per minute of audio is the upper limit.
- **No pictures.** Without a visual anchor, beginners often cannot remember anything from the listening.
- **Playing the audio too fast.** Always slow it down for first exposure. Most beginners benefit from 0.75× or 0.85×.
- **Asking open-ended questions.** "What do you think about…?" is a great discussion prompt **after** the listening, not as a listening task itself.
- **No follow-up.** Listening without a productive follow-up evaporates within minutes.

## Differentiation in mixed-ability classes

In a real classroom, levels vary. Three simple ways to differentiate the same listening activity:

- **Different speeds.** Generate two MP3s of the same dialogue, one at 0.75× and one at 1.0×. Stronger students take the natural-speed version; weaker students take the slower one.
- **Different task complexity.** Stronger students answer open-ended questions; weaker students match pictures or do true/false.
- **Different transcripts.** Stronger students listen without seeing the text; weaker students have the text in front of them with key words highlighted.

None of these require multiple texts. A single dialogue plus a TTS tool with adjustable speed is enough.

## Bringing it together

Listening for beginners is not about harder texts or longer exercises. It is about giving each learner the right amount of support at the right moment: slow enough audio, short enough text, focused enough tasks. Once those three things are right, beginners make visible progress in a single lesson.
